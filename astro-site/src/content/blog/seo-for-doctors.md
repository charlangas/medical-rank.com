SEO for doctors is the work of making your practice show up when someone nearby types "dermatologist near me" or "knee pain specialist" into Google. That's it. The rest is detail.

The detail is where owners get burned. Proposals arrive full of jargon. Reports arrive full of charts. It's hard to tell real work from decoration.

This guide gives you a way to judge SEO without becoming an expert. You'll see what good work includes, how the main options compare, what moves the price, and how to choose. Then two questions most guides skip: what an SEO service costs, and what the "80/20 rule" means for a practice.

## SEO for doctors: what to look for

Picture your practice as a shop on a very long street. Google is the person standing at the corner, pointing people toward the right door when they ask. Search engine optimization (SEO) is everything you do so the pointing goes your way.

Here's the first layer. For local searches, it helps to judge your presence on three questions: relevance (does your listing match what the person typed), distance (how far you are from them), and prominence (how well known and trusted you look).

You can't change distance. You can work on the other two.

So a solid scope of work covers four layers. Think of them as the sign, the shop, the neighbors and the wiring.

### 1. The sign: your Google Business Profile

This is the free listing that shows your hours, phone number, photos and reviews on Google Maps and in the short list of nearby practices at the top of many searches. That list is often called the local pack.

Good work here looks boring. The right primary category. Accurate services. Correct hours, including holidays. Real photos of your office and team. A separate, properly set up listing for each location.

### 2. The shop: your website pages

This is where most practices are thin. A patient searching for a specific treatment wants a page about that treatment. They also want to know who does it, where, which insurance you take, and how to book.

That usually means a page for each main service, a bio page for each provider, and a page for each location that has something real to say.

That last part needs a rule, because location pages are where good intentions turn into spam. Create a location page only when it holds genuinely useful, location-specific information. If you could copy the page, change the city name, and have it fit another office, don't build it.

A location page earns its place when it has things like:

- The street address, parking and directions that only apply to that office
- Hours and phone numbers for that office
- The providers who actually see patients there
- The services and insurance plans that office offers or accepts
- Photos of that office and its entrance

If a second office is just another suite in the same building with the same team and hours, one page may serve both. Don't treat one page per location as a default. Treat it as something each location has to earn.

Google's guidance on [creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) is worth reading yourself. It frames quality around three questions: Who created this? How was it made? Why does it exist? It also points to first-hand experience and expertise. For a medical site, that translates to pages that say who wrote or reviewed them and are written by people who actually do the work.

### 3. The neighbors: reviews and mentions

Prominence is partly what other people say about you. Reviews are the obvious piece. Consistent listings are the quieter one: your practice name, address and phone number (often shortened to NAP) should match everywhere they appear, from directories to your own footer.

Reviews of a medical practice can touch on patient care, so ask your own counsel how your staff should reply before you set a policy.

### 4. The wiring: technical setup and tracking

Google uses programs that visit your site to read it. That's called crawling. Pages it can read may then be eligible to be stored in its library, which is called indexing. Being readable doesn't guarantee Google will index every page. But if a page can't be crawled or indexed, it can't show up.

The wiring also covers whether your site loads and works on a phone, since many patients search from one. And it covers tracking: whether you can see calls, form submissions and appointment requests that came from search.

If nobody can tell you how many appointment requests came from Google, you're flying blind.

### What isn't on the list

Notice what's missing. No secret keywords. No hundreds of blog posts. No pile of links from sites you've never heard of.

Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) name tactics it treats as abuse, including keyword stuffing (cramming terms onto a page), link spam, doorway pages (near-duplicate pages made to catch searches) and scaled content abuse (mass-producing pages to game rankings). The policies say sites that break them may rank lower or not appear at all.

So when you read a proposal, you're checking two things. Does it cover the four layers? And does it avoid the tactics Google calls spam?

## How the main options compare

Think of home renovation. You can do the work yourself, hire one skilled tradesperson, or hire a general contractor who coordinates everyone. All three can produce a good kitchen. Each fails in a different way.

Here's how that maps to SEO.

| Option | What it is | Where it works | Where it tends to break |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do it yourself (you or your staff) | A staff member learns and handles the profile, reviews and website updates | Small practices with one location and someone who likes the work | It competes with front-desk duties and gets dropped when the schedule fills up |
| Freelancer or independent consultant | One person who plans and often does the work | Practices that want direct access to the person doing the work | One person's capacity, and what happens if they disappear |
| Agency that specializes in local search for practices | A company that focuses on local search for medical practices. Team size and structure vary, so check who does what | Multiple locations, competitive markets, owners with no time, as long as the roles on the proposal are real | Roles that exist only on paper, scope that's vague, reports that show activity instead of appointments |
| General marketing agency or web designer's SEO add-on | SEO bundled with design, social media or other services | Practices that want one vendor for everything | SEO is the side dish and may get surface-level attention |
| Software platform | A tool that manages listings, review requests and reporting | Practices that already have someone to act on what it shows | Software shows problems but doesn't fix them or write pages |

A few layers beneath the table.

**DIY is cheaper in cash and expensive in attention.** Updating a Business Profile and running a steady review-request routine are very doable in-house. Keep the routine consistent: the same message goes to every patient after a visit, and staff don't pick who to ask. Ask your counsel which patients or situations should be left out of review requests. Writing accurate, clinically sound service pages and fixing site structure is harder.

**A freelancer gives you a direct line.** The question to ask is who covers the work during vacations or illness, and who holds the logins.

**A specialist agency can bring division of labor, but only if the roles are real.** Don't take "a team" as an answer. Ask which named person handles each of these:

- Content: who writes and edits your pages
- Technical work: who fixes the site and the crawling and indexing issues
- Listing work: who manages your Business Profile and directory listings
- Reporting: who prepares the reports and explains them to you

If the answer to any of those is "the team" or "whoever is available," keep asking. It's also worth asking whether those people would be working on your account or only on the sales call.

**A general agency is convenient.** Just ask to see SEO work specifically, not a design portfolio.

**Software is a tool, not a service.** A dashboard doesn't write a page or answer a review.

### SEO versus Google Ads

You'll hear both pitched together, so here's the difference. Think of renting versus owning.

Google Ads is rented attention. You pay when someone clicks your ad, and the traffic stops when the budget stops. SEO is closer to owning: you invest in your listing and pages, and they can keep showing up without a per-click charge. But owning takes work to build, and nobody can promise exactly where you'll appear.

Many practices use both. Ads can cover a specific service while the organic work builds.

## What makes SEO prices go up or down

Getting an SEO quote is like getting a moving quote. Nobody can price it until they know how many rooms, how many stairs and how far you're going.

Here are the "rooms and stairs" of an SEO project. These are the things that change what you'll be quoted.

- **Number of locations.** Each location needs its own listing and review effort, plus a page of its own if it has something distinct to say.
- **Number of providers and services.** More specialties means more pages to build and keep accurate.
- **How competitive your area is.** A specialty in a dense metro area takes more effort than one with few nearby competitors.
- **The state of your current site.** Technical problems, outdated pages or a site that can't be edited easily add work before progress starts.
- **Who writes the content, and who checks it.** Medical pages need accuracy review. If your physicians review drafts, that's your time. If the provider handles clinical review, that's their time and cost.
- **Cleanup work.** Duplicate listings, wrong phone numbers and old addresses take time to fix.
- **Reporting and meetings.** Simple monthly summaries cost less than detailed custom analysis.
- **Contract structure.** A monthly retainer, a one-time project and hourly consulting are priced differently, and they commit you differently.
- **Who does the work.** Senior specialists cost more than junior staff, and the mix matters.

When two quotes look far apart, one of these is usually the reason. Ask each provider to show you which of these they've included.

## How to choose the right fit

Hiring an SEO provider is a lot like hiring a staff member. You write the job description, check for the right skills, ask good interview questions and check references.

**Start with the job.** Before talking to anyone, write down what you want in plain language. More new patients for a specific service? More calls to a second location? A site that stops sending people to the wrong office? A provider can only be good at a job you've defined.

**Match the scope to your situation.** A single-location practice with a decent site needs different help than a multi-location group with a site that hasn't been touched in years.

**Ask questions that reveal the work.** These are worth asking every provider:

- Which tasks will you do each month, and which will my staff do?
- Who writes the medical content, and who reviews it for accuracy?
- How do you build links or mentions from other sites, and can you show examples?
- Who owns the website, the Business Profile and the analytics account if we part ways?
- How will we know it's working, in calls and appointment requests rather than just rankings?
- What happens to the work if I cancel?
- What do you need from me and my physicians?
- How do you handle anything involving patient information?

**Watch for red flags.**

- A guarantee of a specific ranking or number of patients. Nobody outside Google controls Google.
- Vague scope: "SEO optimization" with no list of deliverables.
- Link-building that sounds like a shortcut, such as bulk links or networks of sites. Compare what they describe to Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies).
- Content produced in bulk with no clinician review. Google's [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks whether you're producing lots of content on many topics hoping some will do well, which is a useful self-check.
- The provider holds the only logins to your accounts.
- Reports full of impressions and rankings but no calls or appointment requests.

**Keep compliance with the right people.** Call tracking, forms, review replies, patient testimonials and any tool that touches patient information are questions for your own counsel and compliance lead. No article can tell you what's allowed for your practice.

**Ask about a starting phase.** Some providers can begin with a defined review of your current setup before you commit to a longer engagement. If they won't, ask why.

## How much does an SEO service cost?

You're probably expecting a number. You won't find one here, because any number printed in a guide would be a guess about a practice that isn't yours.

What you can get is a way to read any quote you receive. Think of an iceberg. The monthly fee is the part above the water. Plenty sits below.

### The part above the water: how providers charge

Most pricing falls into a few shapes.

- **Monthly retainer.** A recurring fee for ongoing work. Fits SEO well because the work is continuous, but you need to know what the retainer covers.
- **One-time project.** A fixed fee for a defined piece of work, such as rebuilding service pages or fixing a site. Clear start and end.
- **Hourly consulting.** You pay for time. Useful if your staff does the work and you want guidance.
- **Hybrid.** A project at the start, followed by a smaller ongoing fee.
- **Pay per lead or call.** Sounds appealing. Get a written definition of what counts: a call, a new patient or a booked appointment. Ask how duplicates, existing patients and wrong numbers are handled.

### The part below the water: costs that never appear on the invoice

- **Your staff's time.** Photos, provider bios, review requests, answering questions from the provider.
- **Physician time.** Reviewing medical content for accuracy.
- **Website changes.** Your web developer may need to fix things the SEO provider finds.
- **Tools and subscriptions.** Some are included, some aren't.
- **Paid ads.** If you pair SEO with Google Ads, your ad spend is a separate line, and it depends on the competition for your service and area.
- **Patience.** SEO builds. You'll pay before you see the full effect, and you should budget with that in mind.

### How to compare quotes

Ask each provider to list deliverables in the same format: what's done, how often, by whom. A cheap quote with a vague scope tends to turn expensive once you start asking for the things you assumed were included. A pricey quote may simply have more written down.

### How to decide what you can afford

Skip "what do other practices pay?" and ask what a new patient is worth to you.

1. Estimate the value of a new patient to your practice over time.
2. Estimate how many additional patients you could realistically see with your current providers and schedule.
3. Be honest about how much of that could credibly come from search.
4. Compare that against the full cost, including the hidden items above.

If your schedule is already full, extra demand is worth less, and the right investment may be the basics done well. If you have open capacity in a high-value service, the math changes.

So don't pick the cheapest or the priciest by default. Pick the one whose written scope matches your goal and whose cost fits your math.

## What is the 80/20 rule in SEO?

Imagine your schedule. A handful of procedure types probably fill most of your calendar and bring in most of your revenue. The long list of everything else you offer matters, but it doesn't drive the week.

That's the 80/20 rule, also called the Pareto principle. It's a rule of thumb: a small share of causes tends to produce most of the results. The numbers 80 and 20 are shorthand. The real split in any situation is rarely that tidy.

For SEO, treat it as a way to decide what to do first, not as a literal formula.

### What it means for a practice

A small set of things tends to matter most, and everything else is secondary until those are right.

- **A complete, accurate Google Business Profile** for each location.
- **A page for each service that drives your schedule.** Not every service you offer. The handful that pay the bills.
- **Reviews that keep coming in.** Fresh, real and from actual patients.
- **A clear path from search to appointment.** A phone number that works, a booking form that doesn't break, a page that loads on a phone.
- **Tracking that shows what's working.**

Notice how short that list is.

### How to find your own 20 percent

You can do this in an afternoon.

1. List your services by how much they matter to your practice, taking both revenue and available capacity into account.
2. Check whether each top service has its own page that answers a patient's real questions.
3. Look at what your analytics show about which pages bring calls and appointment requests. If you don't have that data, fixing the tracking is your first task.
4. Check the Business Profile for each location against reality.
5. Read your recent reviews and see how many are recent.

Whatever is weakest in that list is where the first effort goes.

### What the rule doesn't mean

It doesn't mean ignoring the rest. The long tail of smaller services, patient questions and secondary locations adds up, and in crowded markets the details separate practices. It also doesn't justify skipping the basics to chase a clever trick.

Be wary of anyone selling "the 20% secret." If the list of priorities is a surprise, it probably isn't a priority.

## Frequently asked questions about seo for doctors

### What is the best way to advertise my medical practice?

There isn't one best channel, so pick your first with three checks. Do you have open appointment capacity? If not, more demand won't help. Which service are you promoting? Choose one with openings. Do you need exposure now? If yes, Google Ads can show that service to searchers while your budget runs. If you can wait, strengthen your Business Profile, reviews and service pages first. Track calls and appointment requests either way.

### Who needs SEO?

Any practice that wants new patients who search before they book. Even if most patients come by referral, people often look you up by name before calling, so your listing and reviews still matter. A full schedule in a small market may need only the basics kept accurate. A new practice, a second location, or a crowded specialty in a big metro usually needs more.

### What should a practice check before paying for seo for doctors?

Get the scope in writing: what gets done, how often, and by whom. Confirm you own the website, Business Profile and analytics accounts. Ask how they build links and write content, and who reviews medical claims. Agree on how success is measured, ideally calls and appointment requests. Check cancellation terms. Run anything touching patient information past your own counsel first.
