Marketing medical practice efforts all come down to one question. When someone nearby needs care, do they find you, trust you, and book?

This guide is for owners and practice managers about to spend money, hire someone, or sign with an agency. It covers what to look for, what moves the price, and how to choose, then seven strategy steps, pay, and jobs.

## Marketing medical practice: what to look for

Start with the plain definition, because people ask "what is medical marketing?" and get fog.

Medical marketing is everything you do so that people who need care can find your practice, choose it, and book an appointment.

Here is a mental model: **a map, a sign, and a door.**

- **The map** is where people discover you: Google Maps, your Google Business Profile, and the local results for "dermatologist near me." Work on this is called local SEO. SEO stands for search engine optimization, which means making it easier for Google to understand and show your practice.
- **The sign** is paid advertising. Google Ads lets you pay to appear when someone searches for a service you offer.
- **The door** is your website, specifically the page a person lands on. If it is vague, slow, or buries the phone number, the map and the sign did their work for nothing.

Most disappointing marketing is not a bad map or a bad sign. It is a missing door.

When you evaluate any plan, proposal, or person, look for these:

- **A line from the work to appointments.** Calls, form fills, and booked visits. Not impressions or "brand awareness."
- **Pages built for one service each.** A page for knee pain serves a patient better than one page listing everything you do.
- **Reports you can read in five minutes.** If you need a translator, the report is protecting the vendor, not informing you.
- **Honesty about rankings.** Nobody controls where Google ranks you. Anyone who promises a position is selling a guess.
- **Care with health advertising rules.** Google has a policy page on [healthcare and medicines advertising](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031). Read it yourself, and ask your own counsel what applies to your specialty and state.
- **Content written for people.** Google publishes guidance on [creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

## How the main options compare

One more mental model: **rent versus own.**

Paid ads are rent. You pay, you appear. Stop paying, and you stop appearing.

Your website pages and local listing are closer to owning. You build them, they keep working, and you maintain them.

You want some of each. The mix depends on your market, your budget, and how much ongoing work you can staff.

| Option | Good at | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Do it yourself | Low cash cost, full control | It eats hours, and ad-setting mistakes can waste money quietly |
| One in-house hire | Knows your practice, always available | One person rarely covers writing, SEO, ads, and reporting well |
| Freelancer | Focused skill, flexible scope | Depends on one person's availability and process |
| Local SEO provider | Builds the "own" side: listing, pages, reviews | Needs steady upkeep, and quality varies |
| Google Ads manager | Puts you in front of chosen searches, with spend you control | You rent traffic, and poor landing pages burn budget |
| Full-service agency | Breadth, coordination | Contracts, handoffs, and reports that bury the one number you care about |

No row wins on its own. Use this test instead: can one person own account access, approve priorities, and review inquiries location by location? If yes, a freelancer or small provider may be enough. If nobody can, the gap is ownership, and you need a hire or a provider who takes that role.

Local SEO leaves you assets you keep, but needs upkeep like accurate hours and review replies. Ads need active management and leave you mostly with what you learned about which searches turn into calls. Ask any provider which they handle and which lands on your plate.

## Pricing: what drives the cost

A blog post can't give you a useful price, so be suspicious of any that does. You can learn what moves it.

Think of a car service. **The fee pays the driver. The ad spend is the gas.** They are separate line items, and mixing them up is how owners get surprised.

These are scope factors. An audit of your current listings, pages, and tracking will show which ones apply to you:

- **Number of locations and services.** More of either may mean more listings, pages, ad groups, and tracking to set up and maintain.
- **Market crowding.** A competitive specialty in a dense city may take more work to stand out than a rare specialty in a small town.
- **Content needed.** Writing, photography, and page builds take time.
- **Who does the work.** A senior specialist costs more than a junior one, and a team more than one person.
- **Contract contents.** Setup, management, reporting, calls with you, and call tracking can be bundled or separate.

Ad spend moves with the budget you set, how much competitors bid for the same searches, and how well your landing pages turn clicks into calls.

When you compare proposals, ask each vendor to separate the fee from the ad spend and list what is included. Two quotes that look different may cover different amounts of work.

## How to choose the right fit: questions to ask before you sign or hire

Use the **intake test.**

A good clinician takes a history and examines the patient before writing a prescription. A good vendor does the same. If someone hands you a package before asking about your practice, they are prescribing without an exam.

1. **"What would you look at first in our practice, and why?"** A good answer involves your listing, pages, and current call volume, not a generic package.
2. **"How will we know it's working?"** You want calls and booked appointments, and a plan for counting them.
3. **"Who owns the accounts?"** Your Google Business Profile, Google Ads account, and website should be in your name. If a vendor holds them, leaving gets painful.
4. **"What happens if we stop?"** Understand what you keep and what disappears.
5. **"What can't you control?"** Honest ones say rankings, algorithm changes, and patient decisions.
6. **"How do you handle health advertising rules?"** They should point you to the policies and encourage you to check with counsel.
7. **"Who actually does the work?"** The person on the sales call is not always the person in your account.

A medical marketing course can help you or your practice manager read a report and catch a weak answer, though it doesn't replace doing the work. Check that the syllabus covers account ownership, local listing management, one-service landing pages, call and form tracking, and separating traffic from booked visits. If it is mostly social media tips and branding, it won't help you judge a vendor.

Red flags: guaranteed rankings, guaranteed patients, refusal to give you account access, and reports full of traffic and empty of appointments.

## What are the 7 steps of marketing strategy?

There is no single official list. Versions run from five steps to ten. Here is a practical seven for a medical practice.

The mental model is a **treatment plan.** You diagnose, set a goal, choose treatment, carry it out, and follow up. Skipping the diagnosis is where most plans go wrong.

1. **Set a goal you can count.** "More patients" is a wish. "More new-patient calls for one service" is a goal. Start with one service or one location.
2. **Know who you want to reach.** For each service, identify who decides: the patient, a family member, or a referring provider. Ask your front desk what callers ask before they book.
3. **Audit where you stand.** Search for your services the way a patient would. Check your Google Business Profile for accurate hours, phone, and address. Open your website on a phone. Write down what you find.
4. **Choose channels from what the audit found.** Fix wrong listing details first. Build a page for any service you want to grow before paying for ads that send people there. Fix unanswered calls before adding traffic. Then decide how to split effort between local SEO and ads.
5. **Set a budget and an owner.** Decide the ad spend and who is responsible. A strategy without a named person is a document.
6. **Build and launch.** Write service pages, set up call tracking, publish ads, update listings. Check claims against the advertising policy and your counsel's guidance.
7. **Measure, then adjust.** Review calls and booked appointments on a schedule. Keep what produces visits. Cut or rewrite what doesn't.

Then return to step 1 with a sharper goal. A strategy is a cycle, not a project.

## Medical Marketing Salary

Searching for medical marketing salary figures turns up a wide spread of numbers. The title covers very different jobs.

This guide won't quote a number, because pay varies by location and changes over time. Define the role first, then check current postings and salary surveys for your area.

The mental model is **a pay ladder with several rungs.** Where someone stands depends on:

- **Role and scope.** A coordinator who updates listings sits lower than a manager who owns the budget, vendors, and results.
- **Technical skill.** Running paid search or technical SEO well is specialized, and pay tends to reflect that.
- **Healthcare experience.** Knowing patient flow, referrals, and advertising limits earns trust.
- **Employer and market.** Practice size, employer type (practice, hospital system, agency, freelance), and local market all change pay.

Job titles are loose. "Marketing manager" at one practice is "coordinator" at another. Compare by what the person is accountable for:

| | Coordinator | Manager | Specialist |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accountable for** | Tasks: listings updated, content posted, report assembled | Outcomes: the plan, budget, vendor performance | One craft done well: paid search, SEO, or content |
| **Authority** | Follows a plan. Asks before spending | Sets priorities, approves spend within a limit, directs vendors | Decides how work in their channel is done. Usually not strategy or budget |

To use it, write down three or four tasks this person must own, one outcome they answer for, and whether they can spend ad budget, edit the website, or contact vendors. Then match postings and surveys on duties, not title.

If you are hiring, salary is not the full cost. Add benefits, tools, ad spend, and your time managing the person. If you are considering the career, ask what the role is measured on.

## Medical Marketing Jobs

Medical marketing jobs are a handful of overlapping roles, and many practices combine several into one hire.

The mental model is **a kitchen crew.** Someone plans the menu, someone cooks, someone plates, someone checks the reviews. In a small practice, one person does all four. In a big group, each has a station.

Common roles:

- **Marketing coordinator.** Updates listings, requests reviews, assembles reports.
- **Practice marketing manager.** Owns the plan, budget, and vendors.
- **Digital or paid search specialist.** Handles SEO, website work, or Google Ads campaigns.
- **Content writer.** Writes service pages and patient guides with clinician input.
- **Physician liaison.** Builds relationships with providers who send patients.
- **Patient access or call-handling staff.** Not marketing by title, but the call is where marketing succeeds or fails.

### Which role comes first?

Run four checks before you post a job. The first failure tells you where to start.

| Check | If it fails | Role to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Call your main number at different hours. Is it answered and tracked? | Fix the phone process and call tracking | Patient access or call-handling staff. A marketing hire can't fix a missed call |
| Does each service you want to grow have its own page? | Build pages, with clinician review | Content writer or digital specialist |
| Can you log in as owner to your Google Business Profile, Google Ads, website, and domain? | Get accounts into your name. This is an owner task | Then a coordinator to maintain them |
| Can anyone show last month's inquiries and what produced them? | Set up tracking and a simple monthly report | Coordinator or manager |

If all four pass and results are flat, the bottleneck is likely skill or time in one channel. That is when a specialist, or a manager to direct the whole effort, earns the salary.

### What a candidate should be able to show

A certificate shows someone finished a course, not that they can do the work. Ask for samples with patient information removed, and judge each against what that role controls:

- **Coordinator:** a before-and-after of a cleaned-up listing, and a sample monthly report of calls and forms.
- **Content writer:** two published service pages, and how they got clinician review.
- **Paid search specialist:** a sample account structure, a redacted search terms report showing what they blocked, and how they tracked inquiries.
- **Manager:** a written plan with a goal, an owner, and a budget, and a report that shows something they cut.

Don't hold a listing editor or writer to a booked-appointment number they can't control. If you are looking for work in the field, build samples that show what you did and how you know it worked.

## Frequently asked questions about marketing medical practice

### What is a healthcare marketing specialist?

A healthcare marketing specialist plans and runs marketing for a medical practice, hospital, or health company. That can include local SEO, Google Ads, website content, reviews, and reporting. Ask whether they know Google's [healthcare and medicines advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031). A specialist can't determine whether your ads or data practices are compliant, so confirm privacy and advertising requirements with your own counsel.

### What is medical practice management?

Medical practice management is the business side of running a practice: scheduling, billing, staffing, finances, compliance, vendor decisions, and patient flow. Marketing sits next to it. A well-managed practice turns new inquiries into completed visits, so the two work best when the people behind them talk to each other.
